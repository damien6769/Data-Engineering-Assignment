1. Introduction and Project Overview

1.1 Our Project’s Aim (TBC)

Singapore has experienced a sustained decline in fertility over the past several decades despite numerous policy interventions aimed at encouraging marriage and parenthood. While declining birth counts are frequently reported in the media, such figures describe the outcome rather than the broader demographic, social, and economic changes that may be associated with it.

This project aims to re-examine Singapore’s declining fertility by moving beyond annual birth counts and incorporating additional contextual dimensions. Specifically, we investigate how fertility trends have evolved alongside changes in female labour-force participation, female singlehood during prime childbearing ages, population policies, and selected cultural factors. Rather than attempting to establish direct causal relationships, the project seeks to identify long-term patterns and co-movements that may help explain the persistence of low fertility in Singapore.

Through an improved multi-dimensional visualization, the project aims to transform a simple descriptive chart into a richer analytical representation of Singapore’s demographic transition.

1.2 Source & Context

The original visualization was published by Channel News Asia (CNA) in an article titled “Record number of marriages, drop in Singaporean births in 2022” (September 2023), available at:

https://www.channelnewsasia.com/singapore/marriage-birth-rate-fertility-ageing-population-immigration-2022-3807991

It discusses Singapore’s marriage trends, birth rates, fertility decline, ageing population, and immigration policies. The chart was produced using data from the National Population and Talent Division (NPTD) and focuses on the number of Singapore citizen births between 2012 and 2022.

The topic is highly relevant to Singapore’s long-term demographic challenges. Fertility rates have remained below the replacement level of 2.1 for several decades, contributing to an ageing population, slower natural population growth, and increasing concerns about future workforce sustainability. The visualization was created to support CNA’s discussion of declining births and their implications for Singapore’s population structure.

1.3 Our Chosen Visualization

1.3.1 Visualization Structure and Design

The original visualization selected is a single-panel vertical bar chart titled “Drop in Singapore Citizen Births in 2022”. The chart displays the annual number of Singapore citizen births between 2012 and 2022 and is designed to highlight changes in birth counts over time.

The visualization encodes information using two primary dimensions:

  1. Year — Annual observations from 2012 to 2022 displayed along the horizontal axis.

  2. Number of Singapore Citizen Births — Annual birth counts represented by the height of each vertical bar.

In addition to the two core dimensions, the chart incorporates several supporting design elements:

  • Period grouping — The years are visually divided into two periods, 2013–2017 and 2018–2022, through the use of different colour shades.

  • Average birth annotations — Average birth counts for each five-year period are displayed above the bars to facilitate comparison between the two periods.

  • Direct value labels — Exact annual birth counts are shown above each bar, reducing the need for readers to estimate values from the vertical scale.

1.3.2 The Story It Tells

The visualization communicates a gradual decline in Singapore citizen births over the period shown. Several observations emerge from analysing the bar chart.

Birth counts remain relatively stable throughout most of the period. Annual citizen births fluctuate within a narrow range of approximately 31,000 to 34,000 births, indicating that year-to-year variations are generally modest.

The average number of births decreased between the two highlighted periods. The chart emphasises a decline from an average of 32.7 thousand births during 2013–2017 to 31.8 thousand births during 2018–2022, suggesting a downward shift in birth counts in the later period.

2022 records the lowest birth count in the series. With approximately 30.4 thousand citizen births, 2022 stands out as the lowest value among all years displayed, reinforcing the chart’s message of declining births.

The decline appears gradual rather than abrupt. While annual fluctuations are present, the overall pattern suggests a slow downward movement across the period rather than a sudden change occurring in a single year.

The chart’s use of period averages and colour grouping directs attention towards comparing broader trends between the two five-year periods, rather than focusing solely on individual yearly variations.

1.4 Our Story

Which demographic, social, and economic factors are associated with Singapore’s declining fertility?

Over the past two generations, Singapore’s demographic priorities have reversed. During the country’s early development, rapid population growth was a major policy concern; today, attention has shifted towards persistently low fertility. Singapore’s resident total fertility rate (TFR) fell below 1.0 for the first time in 2023 and declined further to 0.87 in 2025, substantially below the replacement level of approximately 2.1. This project examines the demographic, social, and economic factors associated with that long-term decline.

Singapore experienced a post-war baby boom before introducing increasingly strong family-planning measures. The Stop at Two campaign began in 1972 and formed part of a broader anti-natalist policy environment. TFR reached replacement level in 1975 and continued to decline. By the 1980s, the government had begun reversing its position. The 1983 Great Marriage Debate and the short-lived Graduate Mothers Scheme in 1984 focused public attention on marriage and fertility among university-educated women. In 1987, Have Three or More, If You Can Afford It marked a clearer shift towards pro-natalist policy, followed by the Baby Bonus Scheme in 2001 and later expansions of parenthood support. Despite these interventions, fertility remained below replacement level and continued its long-term decline.

This contrast between sustained policy intervention and persistently low fertility provides the analytical focus of the project. The visualization will not treat policy, employment, or marriage patterns as proven causes. Instead, it will examine how fertility has changed alongside broader structural and cultural developments. Rising female labour-force participation and a growing proportion of women remaining single during prime childbearing ages may be associated with delayed family formation and a shorter period available for having children. Age-specific fertility rates can additionally show whether childbearing has shifted from younger to older maternal age groups. Bringing these measures together allows the project to investigate how Singapore’s fertility decline relates to changes in women’s employment, singlehood, maternal age, policy context, and selected cultural patterns.

2. Critical Analysis of Original Visualization

2.1 Strengths of the Original Visualization

  1. Honest baseline scaling. The vertical axis correctly begins at 0. In bar charts, readers judge magnitude by the physical length of the bars, so starting at 0 avoids artificially exaggerating the drop in births.
  2. Direct value labeling. Every bar displays its exact value at the top, removing the need for readers to trace lines back to the axis to estimate the number.
  3. Clear and direct title. The title “Drop in Singapore citizen births in 2022” communicates the key message within seconds.

2.2 Weaknesses of the Original Visualization

Each weakness is stated with an explanation and a justification.

W1 — Colour saturation illusion

Explanation: The chart uses colour value categorically — a lighter shade for 2013–2017, a deeper purple for 2018–2022, and an even lighter tone for 2012, visually isolating it from both groups. Justification: The darker, more saturated bars attract greater visual attention and may be interpreted as more important or larger, even though they represent the lower-birth period of 2018–2022. The distinctly light 2012 bar reads as a low-value outlier, yet its 33.2k figure is higher than every bar in the darker 2018–2022 block. The intent was categorical, but the execution introduces a potentially misleading visual emphasis.

W2 — Lack of demographic scaling

Explanation: The graph plots absolute raw volume rather than scaling counts against Singapore’s changing demographic structure. Justification: Raw births depend on the size of the reproductive group. If the population of women in prime childbearing ages shrank over the decade, births could fall even if individual fertility behaviour stayed constant or rose. Without normalising the counts into fertility rates, the chart cannot distinguish changes in fertility behaviour from changes in the size and age structure of the female population.

W3 — Limited compositional breakdown by maternal age

Explanation: The chart uses one undivided bar for each year, so it shows annual totals but not how the demographic composition of fertility changes over time, and gives no breakdown by maternal age group. Justification: Readers can compare bar heights, but they cannot identify which maternal age groups contribute to the decline. Adding meaningful age-group segments would shift the visualization from a single total towards a multidimensional account of where the decline is concentrated.

W4 — Short time horizon

Explanation: Birth data is shown only from 2012 to 2022 — roughly an 11-year window. Justification: This captures recent change but not the multi-decade demographic trends shaping Singapore’s fertility landscape. Long-run developments — including rising female education and labour-force participation, as well as major population policies such as the 1984 Graduate Mothers Scheme — predate this window and are invisible here.

W5 — Lack of explicit axis labels and legend

Explanation: Neither axis is explicitly labelled, and there is no legend explaining the colour variations. Justification: Readers must infer that the y-axis is citizen births and that colour marks time periods. Effective visualizations should be self-contained; explicit labels such as “Number of Citizen Births” and “Year” would reduce ambiguity for readers viewing the chart apart from the article.

W6 — Absence of explanatory variables

Explanation: The chart presents a single descriptive metric — births per year — with no variables that could account for the decline. Justification: A single-variable chart answers what happened, not why. The causes of declining births are multi-factorial (behavioural, structural, economic). Without an explanatory layer, the chart is a diagnostic snapshot rather than an analytical tool.

W7 — Compressed variation on the raw-count scale

Explanation: The y-axis correctly begins at zero, but the annual birth counts occupy only the narrow 30.4k–33.7k range near the top of the scale. Justification: Retaining a zero baseline is appropriate for bars, yet the combination of raw counts and a 0–35k axis makes the year-to-year variation difficult to distinguish. Replacing the count-only view with age-specific fertility rates and meaningful stacked segments can reveal changes without truncating the bar-chart baseline.

W8 — Arbitrary period averaging

Explanation: Eleven individual years are collapsed into two 5-year block averages (32.7k vs 31.8k), presenting a 0.9k gap as the headline. Justification: The two-period split is an arbitrary editorial choice, and the 0.9k gap is shown without any measure of variability. Individual years complicate the implied narrative — 2019 (32.8k) exceeds several years in the supposedly higher 2013–2017 block. The averaging produces a cleaner narrative than the annual variation alone supports.

3. Proposed Improvements (Plan Only)

Each improvement targets one or more weaknesses above.

I1 — Use fertility rate, not just raw counts → addresses W2

We plan to replace the count-only view with age-specific fertility rates (ASFR) for the seven maternal age groups from 15–19 to 45–49, while retaining published total fertility rate (TFR) as a summary and validation measure. Rates relate births to the relevant female population, separating fertility behaviour from changes in the size of the reproductive-age population. For example, if the number of births to women aged 30–34 rises over time, this could simply reflect a larger number of women in that age group rather than an actual increase in their likelihood of having children. Using ASFR adjusts for this by measuring births per 1,000 women in that age group, allowing us to determine whether fertility behaviour has truly changed rather than just population structure.

I2 — Use stacked bars with colour mapped to age group → addresses W1, W3

The initial design uses stacked annual bars with year on the x-axis, ASFR on the left y-axis, and maternal age group represented by coloured segments, using a distinct and consistently ordered colour for each of the seven age groups. The combined height summarises the annual level across age-specific fertility rates, while the segments show how each age group contributes, and because colour now maps to age group rather than to editorial time blocks, it no longer implies that the more saturated years are larger simply because they are darker. Continuous contextual variables are provisionally represented as lines so that their direction and long-term movement remain easy to follow.

I3 — Add labels, legend, and annotations → addresses W5

The visualization will include explicit axis titles for Year, ASFR (births per 1,000 women), and Percent (%), together with legends for maternal age groups and the contextual variables. Any policy labels, line styles, background shading, and zodiac markers retained in the final design will also be explained so the visualization remains interpretable without the original article.

I4 — Add female LFPR and singlehood trends → addresses W6

We plan to overlay female labour-force participation rate and the mean proportion of single women across ages 25–39, initially as lines on a shared secondary percentage axis. These variables add socioeconomic and demographic context, allowing their long-term movement to be compared descriptively with the fertility trend.

I5 — Add population-policy context → addresses W6

The initial annotation plan marks the 1972 Stop at Two campaign, the 1987 policy reversal, the 2001 Baby Bonus Scheme, and the 2023 TFR-below-1.0 milestone. The pro-natalist period beginning in 1987 may also be distinguished through background shading. The final annotation style will be refined for readability. These annotations support before-and-after descriptive comparisons without claiming that the visualization alone proves a causal policy effect.

I6 — Extend the historical time horizon → addresses W4

We plan to extend the analysis period from 2012–2022 to roughly 1960–2025, subject to the final availability and consistency of each series. A longer horizon distinguishes temporary fluctuations from long-run trends and situates the decline against major policy shifts: the anti-natalist Stop at Two campaign (1972), the 1987 policy reversal, and the Baby Bonus Scheme (2001), alongside long-run changes in female labour-force participation and singlehood.

I7 — Replace arbitrary period averages with an annual series → addresses W8

Where data is available, the redesign will display annual observations using a consistent structure instead of dividing the data into arbitrary five-year editorial blocks. This preserves annual variation and allows readers to assess long-term patterns without relying on two selected averages.

I8 — Use appropriate zero-based rate and percentage scales → addresses W7

The current plan uses a zero-based ASFR axis for the fertility bars and a clearly labelled secondary percentage axis for female LFPR and singlehood. The precise scales will be refined during implementation, and the transformation and units for both axes will be stated explicitly so readers do not compare the line heights directly with ASFR values. Age-group stacking can make changes in fertility composition visible while preserving an honest zero baseline for the bars.

I9 — Add Chinese zodiac context → addresses W6

Dragon and Tiger years may be marked with distinct symbols, provisionally upward and downward triangles beneath the annual bars. This adds a discrete cultural layer that can be compared with short-term fertility changes while keeping it visually separate from the continuous numerical series.

The seven maternal age groups are 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, and 45–49. Total fertility rate (TFR) is retained as a published summary measure and as a validation variable. It is related to the age-specific rates by the approximation

\[ \text{TFR} \approx \frac{5 \times \sum \text{ASFR}}{1000} \]

because each ASFR represents a five-year age band. In the initial design, TFR is not plotted as a separate line; the stacked bars show the age-specific components underlying the overall fertility level. This treatment may be refined if another representation communicates the relationship more clearly.

4.4 Planned Data Engineering Pipeline

The workflow will be implemented in RStudio as a reproducible sequence of transformations. Raw objects will remain unchanged, and each stage will create a new, clearly named object.

1Stage 1 — Import the Raw Sources

The two SingStat CSV files will be imported with readr::read_csv(). Female LFPR will be requested directly through WDI::WDI(), using Singapore’s country code SGP and the national-estimate indicator. In the final project repository, file paths will be relative to the RStudio project rather than tied to one team member’s computer.

2 Stage 2 — Prepare ASFR and TFR

The fertility table will be filtered to the published TFR row and the seven ASFR rows. pivot_longer() will convert the year columns into observations so that one ASFR row represents one year × age-group combination. The year will be converted to an integer, rate values to numeric, and maternal age group to an ordered factor.

For validation, annual ASFR values will be summed and converted to an approximate TFR. The derived-versus-published comparison will be performed from 1980 onward, when all seven age-group series are available. Earlier years will retain the published TFR, but the incomplete ASFR coverage for ages 45–49 will be documented and will not be treated as a complete reconstruction. Small differences from 1980 onward may occur because the published rates are rounded.

3 Stage 3 — Prepare Female Singlehood

The singlehood table will first be separated into age-group blocks. Age-group labels will be filled down to the nested sex rows, after which only Female observations for ages 25–29, 30–34, and 35–39 will be retained. The wide year columns will then be reshaped into long format.

To reproduce the proposed visualization, the female singlehood measure will be calculated as the unweighted mean of the three published age-specific percentages:

\[ \text{Mean single rate}\_{25-39} = \frac{ \text{Rate}_{25-29}+ \text{Rate}_{30-34}+ \text{Rate}_{35-39} }{3}. \]

The resulting variable will therefore initially be described as the mean proportion of single women across the 25–29, 30–34, and 35–39 age groups, rather than as a population-weighted percentage of all women aged 25–39. A population-weighted calculation may be considered during implementation if suitable female population data is available.

4 Stage 4 — Prepare Female LFPR

The World Bank result will be reduced to year and the female LFPR value, renamed female_lfpr, and filtered to the available 1970–2024 period. Values will remain in their original percentage unit. No unavailable years will be estimated or replaced with zero.

5 Stage 5 — Create Policy and Zodiac Tables

Policy events, the 2023 fertility milestone, the pro-natalist era, and zodiac years will be stored in separate annotation tables rather than repeated across every numerical observation. This keeps annotations independent from the measured fertility and percentage variables.

6 Stage 6 — Align and Join the Annual Drivers

A complete year index from 1960 to 2025 will form the left side of the joins. The female LFPR and singlehood tables will be joined by year, producing one annual driver table. This preserves years outside each source’s coverage as NA, including the singlehood gap in 1981–1982. The ASFR table will remain separate and in long format because it contains seven rows per year.

7 Stage 7 — Prepare the Secondary Percentage Axis

ASFR is measured per 1,000 women, whereas female LFPR and singlehood are percentages. The original percentage variables will be retained unchanged. During implementation, a reversible scale transformation will be calculated from the selected primary and secondary axis ranges so that the two percentage series can be displayed alongside ASFR. The final axis limits and scale factor will be determined after testing the readability of the visualization.

5. Planned Data Analysis

Singapore’s resident fertility has fallen steeply since 1960, dropping below the replacement rate within about fifteen years and reaching a record low total fertility rate of 0.97 in 2023. The original chart we set out to redesign presents this decline as a simple fall in citizen births, which records the outcome but says nothing about its causes. Our central question is therefore why fertility has fallen, and our aim is to bring the decline and its principal drivers into a single figure so they can be read against one another on a shared timeline.

We began exploring data by plotting total fertility against a range of drivers drawn from research and official statistics. These included the median age at first marriage, the median age of mothers at first birth, female labour force participation, educational attainment, the HDB resale price index, household income, the share of never-married residents, birth order, and patterns following the Chinese Zodiac. For each, we examined how the series moved over time relative to fertility and judged whether the data was available over a sufficiently long and consistent period. From this, we selected the dimensions that combine the greatest explanatory weight with usable data and that can coexist in one readable figure. The dimensions chosen are AGe Specific Fertility Rate (ASFR), Female Labour Force Participation (FLFP), and the percentage of single women aged 25-39. It is supported by two contextual layers, the Chinese zodiac and population policies introduced by the government.

The policies are visualised to show the effects of each policy introduced by the government. From there, we can assess and see how well these policies worked overtime, giving us more insight into real driving forces behind Singapore’s declining fertility rate.

We chose the FLFP and numner of single women dimensions to show the cultural shift in Singaporean attitudes towards bearing children. As Singapore modernised, women began attaining higher levels of education and becoming part of the workforce, hence decreasing the number of women having children. Furthermore, with more people spending longer time studying and establishing careers in their twenties, it results in people having kids at a later age. Additionally, more women are choosing to be single so they can focus on their careers instead. This exacerbates the low fertility problem.

On top of that, we also intend to look at things from a cultural angle. Singapore have many traditions and beliefs deeply rooted in superstitution passed down from our ancestors. We wanted to see if the Chinese Zodiac had any particular effect on birth rates for particular years. Any observable patterns can confirm if our hypothesis is correct.

The data is drawn from official sources and imported directly into R. Births and age-specific fertility rates, and the proportion of singles by age and sex, are downloaded as CSV files from the Singapore Department of Statistics and read in with readr. Female labour force participation is obtained more cleanly through the WDI package, which queries the World Bank’s World Development Indicators and retrieves the national-estimate series for Singapore directly from the World Bank’s interface into a tidy data frame. This avoids any manual download, keeps the series reproducible, and is used because it extends the participation figures back to 1970, whereas the local survey series begins only around 1991.

Each source requires preparation before it can be combined. The SingStat tables are stored in wide form with one column per year and several rows of metadata, so we skip the metadata, reshape the tables to long form, extract the year and the age band from the labels, and convert the values to numeric. The singles table is hierarchical, with male and female rows nested beneath each age group, so we carry the age group down and tag each row by sex before filtering to the female rows. For the marriage measure we aggregate the never-married rates for the 25 to 29, 30 to 34 and 35 to 39 bands into a single prime-age series. We also remove the 1999 and 2000 participation values, where a change in the source’s methodology produces a step that is an artefact rather than real movement. The cleaned series are joined on year, and because the two driver series are percentages while the bars are rates, we rescale the percentages onto the fertility axis so they can be shown on one shared secondary axis.

The figure is built as a single layered chart rather than a dashboard, on one shared time axis, so that every series can be compared directly. We use a stacked bar chart for fertility because it carries two messages in one geometry: the total height shows the overall decline, while the coloured segments show the changing age composition, which a single fertility line could not convey. The two drivers are drawn as lines, since a line is the clearest way to read a continuous trend and its slope, and they sit on a secondary percentage axis because they share a common unit. The Chinese zodiac is shown as small triangles below the bars, a discrete marker suited to discrete annual events, with upward triangles for Dragon years and downward triangles for Tiger years to encode the expected direction of effect. Policy is shown in two ways: the background is shaded to separate the anti-natalist era from the pro-natalist era, and individual measures are marked with vertical lines, dotted for the anti-natalist policy and dashed for the pro-natalist ones.

In summary, the year forms the horizontal axis; the age-specific fertility rate is the height of the stacked bars, with the mother’s age group encoded by colour; female labour force participation and the prime-age single rate are lines on the right-hand percentage axis; the Chinese zodiac is the row of triangles beneath the bars; and population policy is encoded by the shaded eras and the vertical policy lines. Together these allow the reader to see the decline, its shift into older ages, its co-movement with rising participation and singlehood, the periodic zodiac effect, and the asymmetry between policy that curbed births and policy that could not restore them.

5.1 What This Analysis Is For

This analysis aims to explain the long-term decline in Singapore’s fertility rate by moving beyond simple descriptive trends to identify and compare key underlying drivers. Rather than only showing how fertility has decreased over time, the goal is to integrate fertility outcomes with demographic, economic, policy, and cultural factors on a shared timeline. This allows us to assess how changes in women’s education and labour participation, partnership formation, housing and social conditions, and government policies may have collectively contributed to declining birth rates. The analysis also explores whether cultural patterns, such as the Chinese Zodiac, show any observable relationship with birth timing.
