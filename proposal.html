1. Introduction and Project Overview

1.1 Our Project’s Aim (TBC)

Singapore has experienced a sustained decline in fertility over the past several decades despite numerous policy interventions aimed at encouraging marriage and parenthood. While declining birth counts are frequently reported in the media, such figures describe the outcome rather than the broader demographic, social, and economic changes that may be associated with it.

This project aims to re-examine Singapore’s declining fertility by moving beyond annual birth counts and incorporating additional contextual dimensions. Specifically, we investigate how fertility trends have evolved alongside changes in female labour-force participation, female singlehood during prime childbearing ages, population policies, and major economic recessions. Rather than attempting to establish direct causal relationships, the project seeks to identify long-term patterns and co-movements that may help explain the persistence of low fertility in Singapore.

Through an improved multi-dimensional visualization, the project aims to transform a simple descriptive chart into a richer analytical representation of Singapore’s demographic transition.

1.2 Source & Context

The original visualization was published by Channel News Asia (CNA) in an article titled “Record number of marriages, drop in Singaporean births in 2022” (September 2023), available at:

https://www.channelnewsasia.com/singapore/marriage-birth-rate-fertility-ageing-population-immigration-2022-3807991

It discusses Singapore’s marriage trends, birth rates, fertility decline, ageing population, and immigration policies. The chart was produced using data from the National Population and Talent Division (NPTD) and focuses on the number of Singapore citizen births between 2012 and 2022.

The topic is highly relevant to Singapore’s long-term demographic challenges. Fertility rates have remained below the replacement level of 2.1 for several decades, contributing to an ageing population, slower natural population growth, and increasing concerns about future workforce sustainability. The visualization was created to support CNA’s discussion of declining births and their implications for Singapore’s population structure.

1.3 Our Chosen Visualization

1.3.1 Visualization Structure and Design

The original visualization selected is a single-panel vertical bar chart titled “Drop in Singapore Citizen Births in 2022”. The chart displays the annual number of Singapore citizen births between 2012 and 2022 and is designed to highlight changes in birth counts over time.

The visualization encodes information using two primary dimensions:

  1. Year — Annual observations from 2012 to 2022 displayed along the horizontal axis.

  2. Number of Singapore Citizen Births — Annual birth counts represented by the height of each vertical bar.

In addition to the two core dimensions, the chart incorporates several supporting design elements:

  • Period grouping — The years are visually divided into two periods, 2013–2017 and 2018–2022, through the use of different colour shades.

  • Average birth annotations — Average birth counts for each five-year period are displayed above the bars to facilitate comparison between the two periods.

  • Direct value labels — Exact annual birth counts are shown above each bar, reducing the need for readers to estimate values from the vertical scale.

1.3.2 The Story It Tells

The visualization communicates a gradual decline in Singapore citizen births over the period shown. Several observations emerge from analysing the bar chart.

Birth counts remain relatively stable throughout most of the period. Annual citizen births fluctuate within a narrow range of approximately 31,000 to 34,000 births, indicating that year-to-year variations are generally modest.

The average number of births decreased between the two highlighted periods. The chart emphasises a decline from an average of 32.7 thousand births during 2013–2017 to 31.8 thousand births during 2018–2022, suggesting a downward shift in birth counts in the later period.

2022 records the lowest birth count in the series. With approximately 30.4 thousand citizen births, 2022 stands out as the lowest value among all years displayed, reinforcing the chart’s message of declining births.

The decline appears gradual rather than abrupt. While annual fluctuations are present, the overall pattern suggests a slow downward movement across the period rather than a sudden change occurring in a single year.

The chart’s use of period averages and colour grouping directs attention towards comparing broader trends between the two five-year periods, rather than focusing solely on individual yearly variations.

1.4 Our Story

Which demographic, social, and economic factors are associated with Singapore’s declining fertility?

Over the past two generations, Singapore’s demographic priorities have reversed. During the country’s early development, rapid population growth was a major policy concern; today, attention has shifted towards persistently low fertility. Singapore’s resident total fertility rate (TFR) fell below 1.0 for the first time in 2023 and declined further to 0.87 in 2025, substantially below the replacement level of approximately 2.1. This project examines the demographic, social, and economic factors associated with that long-term decline.

Singapore experienced a post-war baby boom before introducing increasingly strong family-planning measures. The Stop at Two campaign began in 1972 and formed part of a broader anti-natalist policy environment. TFR fell to around replacement level in 1975, briefly rose to 2.11 in 1976, and remained below replacement level from 1977 onward. By the 1980s, the government had begun reversing its position. The 1983 Great Marriage Debate and the short-lived Graduate Mothers Scheme in 1984 focused public attention on marriage and fertility among university-educated women. In 1987, Have Three or More, If You Can Afford It marked a clearer shift towards pro-natalist policy, followed by the Baby Bonus Scheme in 2001 and later expansions of parenthood support. Despite these interventions, fertility remained below replacement level and continued its long-term decline.

This contrast between sustained policy intervention and persistently low fertility provides the analytical focus of the project. The visualization will not treat policy, employment, marriage patterns, or recessions as proven causes. Instead, it will examine how fertility has changed alongside broader structural and economic developments. Rising female labour-force participation and a growing proportion of women remaining single during prime childbearing ages may be associated with delayed family formation and a shorter period available for having children. Age-specific fertility rates can additionally show whether childbearing has shifted from younger to older maternal age groups. Bringing these measures together allows the project to investigate how Singapore’s fertility decline relates to changes in women’s employment, singlehood, maternal age, policy context, and major economic downturns.

2. Critical Analysis of Original Visualization

2.1 Strengths of the Original Visualization

  1. Honest baseline scaling. The vertical axis correctly begins at 0. In bar charts, readers judge magnitude by the physical length of the bars, so starting at 0 avoids artificially exaggerating the drop in births.
  2. Direct value labeling. Every bar displays its exact value at the top, removing the need for readers to trace lines back to the axis to estimate the number.
  3. Clear and direct title. The title “Drop in Singapore citizen births in 2022” communicates the key message within seconds.

2.2 Weaknesses of the Original Visualization

Each weakness is stated with an explanation and a justification.

W1 — Colour saturation illusion

Explanation: The chart uses colour value categorically — a lighter shade for 2013–2017, a deeper purple for 2018–2022, and an even lighter tone for 2012, visually isolating it from both groups. Justification: The darker, more saturated bars attract greater visual attention and may be interpreted as more important or larger, even though they represent the lower-birth period of 2018–2022. The distinctly light 2012 bar reads as a low-value outlier, yet its 33.2k figure is higher than every bar in the darker 2018–2022 block. The intent was categorical, but the execution introduces a potentially misleading visual emphasis.

W2 — Lack of demographic scaling

Explanation: The graph plots absolute raw volume rather than scaling counts against Singapore’s changing demographic structure. Justification: Raw births depend on the size of the reproductive group. If the population of women in prime childbearing ages shrank over the decade, births could fall even if individual fertility behaviour stayed constant or rose. Without normalising the counts into fertility rates, the chart cannot distinguish changes in fertility behaviour from changes in the size and age structure of the female population.

W3 — Limited compositional breakdown by maternal age

Explanation: The chart uses one undivided bar for each year, so it shows annual totals but not how the demographic composition of fertility changes over time, and gives no breakdown by maternal age group. Justification: Readers can compare bar heights, but they cannot identify which maternal age groups contribute to the decline. Adding meaningful age-group segments would shift the visualization from a single total towards a multidimensional account of where the decline is concentrated.

W4 — Short time horizon

Explanation: Birth data is shown only from 2012 to 2022 — roughly an 11-year window. Justification: This captures recent change but not the multi-decade demographic trends shaping Singapore’s fertility landscape. Long-run developments — including rising female education and labour-force participation, as well as major population policies such as the 1984 Graduate Mothers Scheme — predate this window and are invisible here.

W5 — Lack of explicit axis labels and legend

Explanation: Neither axis is explicitly labelled, and there is no legend explaining the colour variations. Justification: Readers must infer that the y-axis is citizen births and that colour marks time periods. Effective visualizations should be self-contained; explicit labels such as “Number of Citizen Births” and “Year” would reduce ambiguity for readers viewing the chart apart from the article.

W6 — Absence of explanatory variables

Explanation: The chart presents a single descriptive metric, births per year, with no variables that provide demographic, social or economic context. Justification: The chart shows what happened but cannot show how the decline relates to broader changes such as maternal age, female labour-force participation, singlehood, policy periods or economic downturns. Without these contextual layers, it remains a descriptive snapshot rather than a multidimensional analytical visualization.

W7 — Compressed variation on the raw-count scale

Explanation: The y-axis correctly begins at zero, but the annual birth counts occupy only the narrow 30.4k–33.7k range near the top of the scale. Justification: Retaining a zero baseline is appropriate for bars, yet the combination of raw counts and a 0–35k axis makes the year-to-year variation difficult to distinguish. Replacing the count-only view with age-specific fertility rates and meaningful stacked segments can reveal changes without truncating the bar-chart baseline.

W8 — Arbitrary period averaging

Explanation: Eleven individual years are collapsed into two 5-year block averages (32.7k vs 31.8k), presenting a 0.9k gap as the headline. Justification: The two-period split is an arbitrary editorial choice, and the 0.9k gap is shown without any measure of variability. Individual years complicate the implied narrative — 2019 (32.8k) exceeds several years in the supposedly higher 2013–2017 block. The averaging produces a cleaner narrative than the annual variation alone supports.

3. Proposed Improvements (Plan Only)

Each improvement targets one or more weaknesses above.

I1 — Use fertility rate, not just raw counts → addresses W2

We plan to replace the count-only view with age-specific fertility rates (ASFR) for the seven maternal age groups from 15–19 to 45–49, while retaining published total fertility rate (TFR) as a summary and validation measure. Rates relate births to the relevant female population, separating fertility behaviour from changes in the size of the reproductive-age population. For example, if the number of births to women aged 30–34 rises over time, this could simply reflect a larger number of women in that age group rather than an actual increase in their likelihood of having children. Using ASFR adjusts for this by measuring births per 1,000 women in that age group, allowing us to determine whether fertility behaviour has truly changed rather than just population structure.

I2 — Use stacked bars with colour mapped to age group → addresses W1, W3

The initial design uses stacked annual bars with year on the x-axis, ASFR on the left y-axis, and maternal age group represented by coloured segments, using a distinct and consistently ordered colour for each of the seven age groups. The combined height summarises the annual level across age-specific fertility rates, while the segments show how each age group contributes, and because colour now maps to age group rather than to editorial time blocks, it no longer implies that the more saturated years are larger simply because they are darker. Continuous contextual variables are provisionally represented as lines so that their direction and long-term movement remain easy to follow.

I3 — Add labels, legend, and annotations → addresses W5

The visualization will include explicit axis titles for Year, ASFR (births per 1,000 women), and Percent (%), together with legends for maternal age groups and the contextual variables. Any policy labels, line styles, background shading, and recession markers retained in the final design will also be explained so the visualization remains interpretable without the original article.

I4 — Add female LFPR and singlehood trends → addresses W6

We plan to overlay female labour-force participation rate and the mean proportion of single women across ages 25–39, initially as lines on a shared secondary percentage axis. These variables add socioeconomic and demographic context, allowing their long-term movement to be compared descriptively with the fertility trend.

I5 — Add population-policy context → addresses W6

The initial annotation plan marks the 1972 Stop at Two campaign, the 1987 policy reversal, the 2001 Baby Bonus Scheme, and the 2023 TFR-below-1.0 milestone. The pro-natalist period beginning in 1987 may also be distinguished through background shading. The final annotation style will be refined for readability. These annotations support before-and-after descriptive comparisons without claiming that the visualization alone proves a causal policy effect.

I6 — Extend the historical time horizon → addresses W4

We plan to extend the analysis period from 2012–2022 to roughly 1960–2025, subject to the final availability and consistency of each series. A longer horizon distinguishes temporary fluctuations from long-run trends and situates the decline against major policy shifts: the anti-natalist Stop at Two campaign (1972), the 1987 policy reversal, and the Baby Bonus Scheme (2001), alongside long-run changes in female labour-force participation and singlehood.

I7 — Replace arbitrary period averages with an annual series → addresses W8

Where data is available, the redesign will display annual observations using a consistent structure instead of dividing the data into arbitrary five-year editorial blocks. This preserves annual variation and allows readers to assess long-term patterns without relying on two selected averages.

I8 — Use appropriate zero-based rate and percentage scales → addresses W7

The current plan uses a zero-based ASFR axis for the fertility bars and a clearly labelled secondary percentage axis for female LFPR and singlehood. The precise scales will be refined during implementation, and the transformation and units for both axes will be stated explicitly so readers do not compare the line heights directly with ASFR values. Age-group stacking can make changes in fertility composition visible while preserving an honest zero baseline for the bars.

I9 — Add recession years as context → addresses W6

Major recession periods are provisionally represented through shaded intervals or concise annotations rather than as another continuous line. This adds economic context that can be compared with short-term fertility changes without overcrowding the two existing LFPR and singlehood lines.

4.2 Justification for Dataset Selection

A. Births and Fertility Rates

This is the primary dataset because it contains the main fertility outcome variables. Instead of relying only on absolute birth counts, the improved visualization uses age-specific fertility rates (ASFR) to show fertility behaviour across different maternal age groups. This allows us to observe not only whether fertility declined, but also whether childbearing shifted from younger to older age groups over time.

The published Total Fertility Rate (TFR) is retained as a summary measure and validation reference. Since TFR summarises fertility across reproductive ages, it provides a more meaningful indicator of fertility behaviour than birth counts alone.

B. Proportion of Singles

The proportion of single women is included because delayed or reduced marriage formation is closely linked to fertility in Singapore, where most births occur within marriage. The selected female age groups — 25–29, 30–34, and 35–39 — represent key prime childbearing ages. Combining these age groups provides a useful contextual measure of how singlehood patterns have changed during the period when many women would traditionally marry and start families.

C. Female Labour-Force Participation

Female LFPR is included as a labour-market context variable because women’s workforce participation has changed alongside Singapore’s long-term fertility patterns. The current plan compares the mean female labour-force participation rate across ages 25–29, 30–34, and 35–39, the same age groups used for the singlehood measure in B, rather than the all-ages female LFPR. Restricting the measure to prime childbearing ages avoids diluting the comparison with participation trends among teenagers still in education or older women approaching retirement, neither of which bears directly on the project’s delayed-childbearing hypothesis. Comparing this prime-age LFPR with TFR and ASFR allows the project to examine whether changes in employment participation coincide with fertility levels and shifts towards later childbearing. The comparison will remain descriptive and will not treat labour-force participation as a direct cause of fertility decline.

D. Policy and Recession

Population policy events are included as contextual annotations because they represent major turning points in Singapore’s population strategy and provide historical context for interpreting fertility trends. Rather than serving as explanatory variables, these events allow users to visually examine whether noticeable changes in fertility patterns coincide with significant policy interventions or demographic milestones. The selected annotations include the 1972 Stop at Two campaign, which aimed to reduce population growth; the 1987 “Have Three or More, If You Can Afford It” policy reversal, marking Singapore’s shift from anti-natalist to pro-natalist policies; the 2001 Baby Bonus Scheme, which introduced financial incentives to encourage parenthood; and the 2023 milestone where the resident Total Fertility Rate fell below 1.0, highlighting the severity of Singapore’s fertility decline.

Major recession periods are included as contextual annotations because economic uncertainty may influence decisions relating to marriage and childbearing. During periods of recession, households may postpone long-term financial commitments due to concerns about employment security, income stability, housing affordability, and future economic prospects. While recession periods are not treated as direct causes of fertility changes, annotating them enables users to examine whether short-term fluctuations in fertility coincide with major economic downturns. The selected periods are the 1984–1985 recession, the 1998 Asian Financial Crisis recession, the 2001 dot-com recession, the 2008–2009 Global Financial Crisis recession, and the 2020 COVID-19 contraction.

Because changes in marriage or conception decisions may only appear in annual birth data after several months, fertility will be compared during each downturn and approximately one year afterward. Any observed timing or co-movement will be interpreted descriptively and not as evidence that a recession caused a fertility change.

5. Planned Data Engineering Pipeline

5.1 Workflow Stages

The workflow will be implemented in RStudio as a reproducible sequence of transformations. Raw objects will remain unchanged, and each stage will create a new, clearly named object.

1Stage 1 — Import the Raw Sources

All three SingStat files will be imported as raw data sources.. In the final project repository, file paths will be relative to the RStudio project rather than tied to one team member’s computer.

2 Stage 2 — Prepare ASFR and TFR

The fertility table will be filtered to the published TFR row and the seven ASFR rows. pivot_longer() will convert the year columns into observations so that one ASFR row represents one year × age-group combination. The year will be converted to an integer, rate values to numeric, and maternal age group to an ordered factor.

For validation, annual ASFR values will be summed and converted to an approximate TFR. The derived-versus-published comparison will be performed from 1980 onward, when all seven age-group series are available. Earlier years will retain the published TFR, but the incomplete ASFR coverage for ages 45–49 will be documented and will not be treated as a complete reconstruction. Small differences from 1980 onward may occur because the published rates are rounded.

3 Stage 3 — Prepare Female Singlehood

The singlehood table will first be separated into age-group blocks. Age-group labels will be filled down to the nested sex rows, after which only Female observations for ages 25–29, 30–34, and 35–39 will be retained. The wide year columns will then be reshaped into long format.

To reproduce the proposed visualization, the female singlehood measure will be calculated as the unweighted mean of the three published age-specific percentages:

\[\text{Mean single rate}\_{25-39} = \frac{ \text{Rate}_{25-29}+ \text{Rate}_{30-34}+ \text{Rate}_{35-39} }{3}. \]

The resulting variable will therefore initially be described as the mean proportion of single women across the 25–29, 30–34, and 35–39 age groups, rather than as a population-weighted percentage of all women aged 25–39. A population-weighted calculation may be considered during implementation if suitable female population data is available.

4 Stage 4 — Prepare Female LFPR

The labour force table will be filtered to Female observations for ages 25–29, 30–34, and 35–39, then reshaped from wide year columns into long format, following the same treatment as the singlehood table in Stage 3. The female LFPR measure will be calculated as the unweighted mean of the three age-specific participation rates, renamed female_lfpr_25_39. As with the singlehood measure, this is an unweighted mean across the three age groups rather than a population-weighted rate for all women aged 25–39, and may be revisited if suitable female population data becomes available. Values will remain in their original percentage unit, and no unavailable years will be estimated or replaced with zero.

5 Stage 5 — Create Policy and Recession Tables

Policy events, the 2023 fertility milestone, the pro-natalist era, and recession years will be stored in separate annotation tables rather than repeated across every numerical observation. This keeps annotations independent from the measured fertility and percentage variables.

6 Stage 6 — Align and Join the Annual Drivers

A complete year index from 1960 to 2025 will form the left side of the joins. The female LFPR and singlehood tables will be joined by year, producing one annual driver table. This preserves years outside each source’s coverage as NA, including the singlehood gap in 1981–1982. The ASFR table will remain separate and in long format because it contains seven rows per year.

7 Stage 7 — Prepare the Secondary Percentage Axis

ASFR is measured per 1,000 women, whereas female LFPR and singlehood are percentages. The original percentage variables will be retained unchanged. During implementation, a reversible scale transformation will be calculated from the selected primary and secondary axis ranges so that the two percentage series can be displayed alongside ASFR. The final axis limits and scale factor will be determined after testing the readability of the visualization.

5.2 Planned Cleaning and Transformation Decisions

The main cleaning decisions are to standardise year and age-group fields, reshape the wide annual tables into tidy long form, preserve unavailable observations as NA, and avoid automatic interpolation. The three female singlehood age-group percentages will initially be combined using an unweighted mean and labelled accordingly. Each source will retain its published unit, and any secondary-axis transformation will be reversible and used only for plotting.

5.3 Target Output Schema

The engineering workflow will produce separate tables for the long-form ASFR observations, annual TFR validation, annual LFPR and singlehood drivers, policy and fertility milestones, and recession periods. Keeping measured series and contextual annotations separate reduces duplication and allows the final visualization design to be refined without changing the cleaned source values.

6. Planned Data Analysis

6.1 What This Analysis Is For

Singapore’s resident fertility has fallen steeply since 1960, dropping below the replacement rate within fifteen years. The resident total fertility rate first fell below 1.0 in 2023 and declined to a record low of 0.87 in 2025. The original chart selected for redesign shows the decline in citizen births but does not provide the broader context associated with it.

This analysis aims to examine the long-term decline in Singapore’s fertility rate by moving beyond simple descriptive trends to identify and compare key associated factors. Rather than only showing how fertility has decreased over time, the goal is to integrate fertility outcomes with demographic, economic, and policy context on a shared timeline. This allows changes in women’s labour participation, partnership formation, economic conditions, and government policies to be compared with fertility trends. The analysis also explores whether major recession periods show any observable relationship with short-term fertility patterns.

We began exploring data by plotting total fertility against a range of factors drawn from research and official statistics. These included the median age at first marriage, the median age of mothers at first birth, female labour-force participation, educational attainment, the HDB resale price index, household income, the share of never-married residents, birth order, and major economic downturns. For each, we examined how the series moved over time relative to fertility and judged whether the data was available over a sufficiently long and consistent period. From this, we selected dimensions that combine explanatory relevance with usable data and that can coexist in one readable figure. The selected numerical dimensions are age-specific fertility rate (ASFR), female labour-force participation rate (LFPR), and the mean singlehood rate among women aged 25–39. They are supported by two contextual layers: major population policies and recession periods.

The policy annotations allow fertility patterns before and after major policy changes to be compared descriptively. They provide historical context for the long-term trend but will not be treated as evidence that a policy caused a particular fertility outcome.

We selected female LFPR and female singlehood to examine broader social and labour-market changes associated with delayed family formation. As Singapore modernised, women attained higher levels of education and participated more extensively in the workforce. Longer periods spent studying and establishing careers may coincide with later marriage and childbearing. These relationships will be interpreted as associations rather than direct causal effects.

The project also considers fertility from an economic perspective. Some short-term changes in fertility appear near periods when Singapore experienced major recessions, including a dip across 1998–1999 around the 1998 recession. We therefore plan to annotate major downturns and compare fertility during the recession period and approximately one year afterward. Employment and income uncertainty may influence decisions about marriage and childbearing, but any observed co-movement will be treated as descriptive rather than causal.

The data is drawn from official sources and will be prepared in R. Births and age-specific fertility rates, and the proportion of singles by age and sex, are obtained from the Singapore Department of Statistics. Female labour-force participation is obtained from the World Bank’s ILO modelled-estimate series for Singapore. The selected sources will be imported and transformed reproducibly in the final RStudio project.

Each source requires preparation before it can be combined. The annual tables will be reshaped into long form, year and age-group fields will be standardised, and values will be converted to the appropriate numerical types. The singlehood table is hierarchical, with male and female rows nested beneath each age group, so the age-group labels will be carried down before filtering to female observations. The never-married rates for ages 25–29, 30–34 and 35–39 will be combined into one mean prime-age series. The cleaned series will be aligned by year while retaining unavailable observations as NA.

The figure is planned as a single layered chart rather than a dashboard, using one shared time axis so that the series can be compared directly. Stacked bars are proposed for fertility because they carry two messages in one geometry: the combined height summarises the annual fertility level, while the coloured segments show changing age composition. The two continuous contextual variables are provisionally represented as lines on a secondary percentage axis because they share a common unit. Recession periods may be shown through markers or shaded intervals, while population policies may be indicated through labelled vertical lines and background shading. These design choices will be refined during implementation for readability.

In summary, year forms the horizontal axis; ASFR is represented by stacked bars, with maternal age group encoded by colour; female LFPR and the prime-age singlehood rate are contextual series on a percentage axis; recession periods provide economic context; and population policies are represented through annotations. Together, these layers are intended to show the long-term decline, its shift towards older maternal ages, its co-movement with participation and singlehood, and its timing relative to policy changes and economic downturns.

6.3 Planned Analyses

The analysis will use descriptive trend comparisons to examine how Singapore’s fertility patterns have changed and how these changes coincide with selected demographic, social, economic and policy factors. The results will be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of direct causation.

Long-term fertility trend and maternal-age shift

Annual TFR and ASFR will be examined across the available study period to identify major turning points and longer-term changes. Where appropriate, changes may also be summarised by decade or selected policy period. The contribution of each maternal age group will be compared over time to determine whether fertility has shifted from younger age groups, particularly ages 20–29, towards older age groups such as 30–39.

Female LFPR and fertility

Female LFPR will be compared with fertility over their overlapping years to identify whether rising labour-force participation coincides with lower fertility or a shift towards later maternal ages.

Female singlehood and fertility

The mean singlehood rate among women aged 25–39 will be compared with TFR and ASFR. A descriptive correlation may be calculated over the overlapping period, provided that missing years are handled transparently. Correlation will not be interpreted as proof that singlehood caused fertility to decline.

Policy-period comparison

Selected policy milestones will be placed on the fertility timeline to compare patterns before and after their introduction. These include the 1972 Stop at Two campaign, the 1987 policy reversal and the 2001 Baby Bonus Scheme. Because other demographic and economic changes occurred simultaneously, these comparisons will not be treated as estimates of policy effectiveness.

Recession-period and lag comparison

Fertility patterns will be compared during major recession periods and approximately one year afterward. Particular attention will be given to the fertility dip across 1998–1999 around the 1998 Asian Financial Crisis recession. Other periods considered include 1984–1985, 2001, 2008–2009 and 2020. These comparisons will provide economic context but will not establish that recessions caused the observed fertility changes.

Interpretation of results

The analysis will focus on long-term trends, timing, co-movement and descriptive correlations. Results will be interpreted cautiously because fertility may also be influenced by factors not included in the visualization, such as housing, household income, childcare costs, personal preferences and broader social changes.
